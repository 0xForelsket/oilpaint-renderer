# Making oilpaint look like a photograph of a real oil painting: market and science research

Prepared 2026-09-30 for Sean. Read-only research; the only file written in the repository is this report. Context read first: `docs/plans/LIBRARY_PLAN.md`, `docs/plans/ENGINE_PLAN_v1.md`, `docs/STYLE_REFERENCE.md`, and the native-resolution Storm Light images (`../storm-light-painting/painting/`).

**How to read the evidence tags.**
- **[V]** verified: I fetched and read the source (page or PDF), or ran the check myself.
- **[S]** search snippet or listing only: the source exists but I did not read its body (often a 403 or truncated page).
- **[I]** my inference or synthesis; not a claim from a source.
- **[measured]** I measured it on Storm Light with a script (files under `../storm-light-painting/`).

## 1. Executive summary: the ten most important gaps, ranked by expected visual impact

The short version. oilpaint's *paint* (bristle lanes, pick-up, spectral mixing, settling planned) is already ahead of most products on the parts that are hard to fake. What separates its output from a photo of an oil painting is mostly **how the finished surface is lit and finished, and how the picture's decisions vary across the canvas**. Neither is in any milestone of `LIBRARY_PLAN.md` (L7 and L11 cover paint physics; nothing covers the optical finish or a photographic stage). I measured the current light: it is a satin emboss, with about 5% of pixels shifted by more than 20/255 in either direction and no cast shadows (section 6). Perception studies add a design rule: bumpy relief and gloss are judged together, and paintings show sharper, higher-contrast highlights and 2-5 times more local texture variation than renders (section 4.8).

1. **Photographic relief lighting: cast shadows, ambient occlusion, a physically based specular lobe (roughness, Fresnel, soft-box highlight).** Every leader converged here (Rebelle 8 shipped RealShader and SoftShadows in 2025; Stuyck 2017, AR²O Painter 2025 and an April 2026 paper use measured-BRDF or microfacet/GGX models), and the failure users name for Fresco, Painter and Krita is impasto that reads as a bump map with a drop shadow. IMPaSTo's own lighting (Blinn-Phong, no shadows or occlusion) is what oilpaint has. Exact and deterministic on a CPU (height-field shadow and horizon sweeps). **Not planned. Cost M.**
2. **Surface state and varnish: per-area gloss (wetness, thickness, oil content), a Saunderson-style surface term, a final varnish level, and darks that go below L\* 20.** Varnishing is modelled as removing the surface term (Berns); Berns and de la Rie found that whether the varnish surface matches the paint's roughness matters about 20 delta E00 while the varnish's refractive index matters about 1; uniform gloss reads as plastic (Delft). Ochrell explicitly excludes surface reflection. The current global `L_floor = 20` sits at the *matte* floor (about L\* 24-26 with 4% surface white) while a varnished off-specular dark can reach L\* 7 (my arithmetic), and the Storm Light darkest 0.1% sit at L\* 19.3. **Not planned. Cost S-M.**
3. **An optical paint film: real glazes, scumbles, translucency, and thickness-dependent colour that lets ground and lower layers show through.** Chu et al. name thinned semi-transparent paint as missing, and 13 artists ranked multi-layer pigments first (4.5/5). **Planned (F0 spike, L11). Cost L.** The research supports "go".
4. **Relief that behaves like paint, tied to tone: thick lights and thin darks, settled edges, real knife planes, flat/filbert/round shapes.** Painters' rule of thumb is impasto in the lights and thin darks; oilpaint's `hgain` and `relief` are not tied to tone. The tone coupling is planner-only. **Settling, brushes and knife are planned (L7, L10, L12); tone coupling is not. Cost S for the coupling, M for the rest.**
5. **Value range and colour truth: real-pigment calibration, deep transparent darks, limited-palette and colour-temperature logic.** Ochrell has no real-paint calibration, Storm Light's 99th-percentile chroma is C\* 43.6 (a pastel key), and painters work from a handful of earth-dominated tubes. **Not planned. Cost M-L.**
6. **An edge and focus hierarchy (lost and found edges) and a value-first design pass in the planner.** Painters keep hard edges only near the focus; paintings have more colour-only edges than photos; Photoshop's filter is judged fake because it treats every pixel alike. **Partly present (`spill`, `hardness`, smudge layer). Cost M.**
7. **The canvas as a physical object: weave at a calibrated scale that shows through thin paint and dry-brush, an unpainted margin, stretched-edge and gallery-wrap side, frame shadow.** The weave was not visible in the native crops I inspected, and no milestone models the edge. Real numbers now exist: thread pitch about 0.63-0.75 mm, relief up to 1.1 mm on Van Gogh-style test paintings, brush-mark width 0.6 mm (about 2.4 px at 2400 px on a 600 mm painting, which fits the 1-3 px ridge width in `docs/STYLE_REFERENCE.md`). **Cost S-M.**
8. **Micro-structure of the paint body at 100% zoom: opaque thick paint that hides what is under it, tearing and dragged ridges at stroke ends, embedded hairs, pigment inhomogeneity.** My native crops read as smooth translucent ribbons with fine parallel streaks; Chu et al. attribute the real-oil look to preserved fine detail, and Delanoy et al. measured 2-5 times higher local texture variation in paintings than in renders. **Partly planned (L7). Cost M.**
9. **A camera stage: light falloff, vignette, fixed-seed noise, lens softness, sharpening.** Cheap, and it moves the read from "render" to "photo". **Not planned. Cost S.**
10. **No realism measurement.** There is no A/B set of real museum crops against renders, so realism changes cannot be judged. I found no published study to borrow. Build a 20-vs-20 crop test at 100% and add two statistics (edge-sharpness distribution, dark-value histogram). **Not planned. Cost S.**

Also worth deciding, but lower impact: craquelure and ageing (optional; **M**), and drips or tilt (low). Learned "neural refinement" is **not recommended** for this engine: it breaks the cross-host determinism guarantee, and the learned painters I read (2018-2026) mostly improve stroke *placement*; only two 2025-26 works add a height field and PBR relight, one without an ablation (section 4.7).

**Licence flags (section 7).** Adobe holds four US patents on brush paint transport that Google Patents lists as active, with expiries in 2031: a two-buffer reservoir-plus-pick-up brush (US 8,462,173 and US 8,599,213), loading a brush from the canvas (US 8,654,143) and a paint-volume fill channel (US 8,749,572). The first two are described in terms that match oilpaint's lane "dirt" plus paint load. I could not read any claim text. Get them read by someone qualified before any release. Mixbox stays opt-in (CC BY-NC). Among neural painters, FRIDA (GPL-3.0), Stylized Neural Painting (non-commercial) and several unlicensed repos cannot be reused; Learning to Paint (MIT), Paint Transformer (Apache-2.0) and Im2Oil (CC0) can, but they do not model surface.

### 3.2 Product notes

**Rebelle (Escape Motions)** — proprietary perpetual licence, no subscription. Standard $89.99, Pro $149.99 at the Rebelle 7 launch; Rebelle 8 released 2 Oct 2025, 8.3 in mid-2026 (the vendor page and CG Channel differ by two days). **[V]**
- *Marketed realism:* oil and acrylic impasto with a heightmap export (16-bit EXR since 8.3); **RealShader** (Pro): lighting from real-photograph environment maps, per-layer reflectivity and granulation, custom reflection textures; **SoftShadows** (Pro): ray-traced shadows cast by raised strokes, strength 0-10 and shadow altitude 0-10, off by default; **Bristle Brushes** (Pro, 8.0): a particle system where each strand moves and reacts to colour and pressure; **pigment mixing** (Pro): Mixbox-based Kubelka-Munk, per-layer toggle; **NanoPixel** (Pro): machine-learning upscaling, up to 20x (32k exports); 180+ paper and canvas textures; Rebelle 7 added metallics and ray-traced analysis of the paper height map so strokes respond to weave. **[V]** escapemotions.com about page, blogs, manual 7 PDF and manual 8 pages, cgchannel 2023 and 2026, digitalproduction 2025.
- *How, where known:* strokes carry a height structure; Visual Settings hold global Impasto Depth (0-10), Gloss (0-10), Paint Texture and Paper Texture (0-10 each), also per layer. Oil properties: Loading, Oiliness, Length (paint runs out), MultiColored, Dirty Brush (carries traces of picked-up colour). Pen pressure switches between laying paint and mixing. Oils do not cure; the wet/dry system is mostly about water. **[V]** manuals. My inference: the shading is image-based light plus ray-cast shadows over a 2.5D height field, without a glaze, varnish or subsurface layer model. **[I]**
- *Reception:* praise for oils, acrylics and gouache as the most realistic the reviewer had used (a Rebelle 7 review; vendor-friendly forum users call the paint "buttery" against Painter). Complaints are about performance and behaviour, for example impasto brushes lag on large canvases, lower-layer impasto that upper layers never cover, and metallics that work with only some oil brushes. I found **no independent critical review of RealShader's visuals** (the TechRadar and Creative Bloq bodies were truncated). **[V]** with the caveat above.

**Corel Painter** — newest release **Painter 2023** (June 2022), subscription or perpetual; Corel staff said in January 2024 that no major new features were planned for 2024, and I found no 2024-2026 release. **[V]** (appaddict review May 2026, learn.corel.com).
- *Features:* Thick Paint (bristle brushes and palette knives, presets Thick, Soft, Thin, Dry, paint load 0-100%), RealBristle (spread and twist follow pressure, tilt, bearing), Impasto (a height map lit by a virtual light source, per Corel's help text), Real Wet Oils, Fluid Paint (2023: cover, opacity and glazing, GPU accelerated), auto-painting from photos (strokes follow forms; randomness sliders; works from a cloned underpainting). **[V]** product.corel.com help; painterartist.com.
- *Impasto history:* Painter 5 introduced Impasto in 1996 (height map plus light); Thick Paint arrived in 2018. **[S]** learn.corel.com.
- *Fake-look evidence:* one user says impasto edges are hard and pixelated, and that impasto looks like blobs of colour with a drop shadow; certain brushes only work on certain layers; Corel's own performance advice is to disable High Quality Display, remove ambient lighting, increase droplet spacing and reduce brush density. **[V]** (the user quotes are from a competitor's forum thread).
- *Patents:* none found on RealBristle, Thick Paint or Impasto in my searches (not systematic). **[I]**

**ArtRage (Ambient Design)** — ArtRage Vitae ($80, "think of it as ArtRage 7"); ArtRage 6 ($59, 2019). Proprietary. **[V]**
- *Features:* oil brush with Loading, Thinners, Insta-Dry, Auto Clean, Stiffness; adjustable Impasto and Gloss; canvas "lit like a 3D surface" with adjustable light angle and intensity; wet paint mixes with wet paint, dry paint does not, wet over dry does not interact; a "realistic" pigment-style colour mix option (technique undocumented). **[V]** artrage.com pages; my inference is that layered work breaks the illusion, since the vendor's own testimonial praises "single-layer" work. **[I]**
- *Chu et al. 2010* say ArtRage 3 sampled paint coarsely on smearing and rendered strokes by sweeping a 1D texture, which loses detail. Old, unverified for 2026. **[V]**

**Adobe Fresco and Photoshop Mixer Brush** — Fresco is freemium on iPad and Windows, released Nov 2019, still updated in 2026. **[V]** (Wikipedia release table; a snippet claiming a later version was unconfirmed).
- Live Brushes are pixel-based and physics-driven; oils never dry. The lineage is **Project Wetbrush** (Chen, Kim, Ito, Wang 2015, with NVIDIA): 3D ridges, too heavy for consumer hardware, stayed a demo. That Live Oil derives from Wetbrush is my inference. **[V]/[I]**
- **Fake-look evidence:** the Adobe community thread where users dislike the fake, bump-map-like impasto and Adobe staff confirm there is no toggle; Nelson Chu says its oil is stamp-based, shows less streak detail than Fresh Paint and mixes blue and yellow to grey (competitor, biased). **[V]**
- Photoshop's **Mixer Brush**: Wet, Load and Mix parameters, reservoir and pick-up wells; its limitation is that it is a 2D pixel brush without height or light. **[S]** Adobe help snippet, and my reading of the Adobe patent family (section 7).

**Procreate (Savage Interactive)** — one-time purchase; iPad 5.4.x (2026). The handbook documents Wet Mix (Dilution, Charge, Attack, Pull, Grade, Blur, Wetness Jitter) and Grain modes. **No impasto, height or lighting controls are documented.** Stuyck et al. (2017) put it in the row with no simulation, flat 2D painting and no light interaction. An artist notes that digital work tends to come out too precise, too smooth and too heavy, and fakes texture with Multiply and Overlay canvas layers. **[V]** help.procreate.com; kamilastankiewicz.com; Stuyck Table 4. Third-party "impasto" brush sets are sold widely; they are texture stamps, not simulation **[I]**.

**MyPaint / libmypaint** — libmypaint is **ISC**; the MyPaint application is GPL-2.0-or-later. Smudge uses colour "buckets" (256) that carry picked-up colour; no height or impasto model. **[V]/[S]**

**Krita** — GPL-3.0-only. Colour Smudge engine (smear copies the area under the last dab), a heightmap paint-thickness mode since 5.0, bristle engine with heightmap tips. No Mixbox because of the licence; developers are pursuing MyPaint-style spectral mixing. Artists on the forum say impasto does not scale like real paint and RGBA tips show dab gaps. Ideas only for oilpaint. **[V]**

**Paintstorm Studio** — perpetual licence; smart "Dirty" mode, real palette mixing, bristle brushes of any form. No height or lighting model on the vendor page; reviews call it powerful and laggy. **[V]/[S]**

**Microsoft Fresh Paint and Project Gustav** — Gustav began in 2010; Chu, Baxter, Wei and Govindaraju's NPAR 2010 paper is the technical core; shipped as Fresh Paint on 25 May 2012, still in the Microsoft Store, site archived in 2017. Stuyck et al. rate its brush behaviour "++" but note no simulation from dilution and no varying light. **[V]**

**Expresii** — Nelson Chu's watercolour app (2017), CFD plus 3D brushes; its blog argues stamp-based brushes cannot make organic streaks. **[V]**

**Clip Studio Paint, Realistic Paint Studio, Artweaver** — oil looks come from same-layer colour mixing, texture stamps and paid brush packs; not simulation. Realistic Paint Studio (iOS) is praised for feel but its 5000x5000 canvas lags and primary blending is off. **[V]/[S]**

**Reading (inference).** No mass-market automatic tool renders paint physically: they filter pixels. The only physical-plus-automatic system I found is the research prototype AR²O Painter (2025, portraits only). That leaves an open niche for oilpaint: automatic planning plus physical paint plus a photographic finish.

### 3.4 Research and open-source automation

- **p5.brush** (acamposuribe): **MIT**, WebGL2, seeded through p5's `randomSeed` and `noiseSeed`, watercolour fills after Tyler Hobbs, credits spectral.js for colour blending, no oil or impasto. **[V]**
- **Hertzmann 1998 and painterJava (MIT)**: the layered, error-driven curved-stroke algorithm oilpaint's planner descends from. **[V]**
- **Neural painters:** Learning to Paint (MIT), Paint Transformer (Apache-2.0), Stylized Neural Painting (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0, has an oil-brush renderer). The neural stroke renderers are learned approximations, not physical paint. **[V]**
- **The Painting Fool** (Colton): creative-AI painter; no media simulation detail found. **[V]**
- **e-David (Konstanz, Deussen)**: a robot painter with a camera feedback loop (photo, difference from target, new strokes); five brushes and 24 colours in a 2013 article. Its 2018 paper says the robot held brush distance and pressure constant so stroke width came only from changing brushes, and finesse was lacking; its later region-based work aims for more human-like layered painting. Wikipedia notes that pre-defined strokes give very clean but "robotic" results and dynamically generated strokes leave detailed areas too dark. **[V]/[S]** (kops.uni-konstanz.de, Wikipedia, edavid.de). The Konstanz group also released the MIT `painty` library (KM paint, brushes, palette extraction) **[V]**.
- **Robotic oil painting 2026:** IMPASTO (Stanford: Wang, Li, Zhu, Yu, Goldberg, Fei-Fei, Wu, Li, Zhang; arXiv 2603.29315) learns pixel dynamics from robot self-play and plans forces and colours on a 7-DoF arm; FRIDA (CMU, 2022) uses a differentiable real-to-sim-to-real simulator. Neither models thickness lighting as a rendering goal in the abstracts. **[V]**

2. *Layered pigment beats a single mixed state.* Stuyck's artists put it first, and Chu's future work asks for it. This is the F0 film spike's question, and the literature favours a "yes" **[V]/[I]**.
3. *Lighting is what separates 2004-style paint from 2025-style paint.* Stuyck (Torrance-Sparrow fitted to measurements, IBL), AR²O (microfacet plus Fresnel) and Rebelle 8 (RealShader plus SoftShadows, section 3) all moved in the same direction within the last decade **[V]/[S]**.

**What "impasto looks fake" means in optical terms (inference).** The failure modes users report (an emboss with a drop shadow, no matching gloss) are what a *Lambert-plus-bump* model produces. Real paint adds four visible things: (1) a specular lobe whose strength depends on wetness, thickness and varnish, with Fresnel at grazing angles; (2) **cast shadows** from ridges at low light angles, the reason painters photograph with a raking light; (3) **ambient occlusion** in furrows and at stroke edges; (4) light that enters thick pale paint and leaks a little past its edge. oilpaint has (1) in a simple form and a stand-in for (3). Section 4.8 adds that bumpy relief and gloss are judged together by viewers, so the highlight and the shading have to agree. **[I]**

**Why oil darkens and saturates pigments (inference from Wikipedia *Hiding power*).** In air the contrast between pigment and air is large, so scattering is strong and a powder looks pale. In oil the contrast shrinks, scattering drops and absorption dominates; pigments with n near 1.5 (extenders) go nearly transparent. Mixbox warns that its K and S come from glossy acrylic and so bake in a rich, saturated look that overstates saturation for matte media **[V]** (Sochorová and Jamriška's paper, read by the optics helper).

**What this means for the floor of the darks (my calculation, using the optics helper's arithmetic).** The lowest value a dry surface can show depends on the viewing geometry. For a K-M reflectance R_K = 0.02: with the specular component excluded (viewing off the specular direction of a smooth glossy varnish) L\* is about **7**; with a rough matte surface adding 4% white it is about **26**. A pure 4% surface gives Y = 0.04 and L\* about 24. oilpaint's `L_floor = 20` therefore sits at or just below the *matte* floor, and the measured Storm Light darkest 0.1% (L\* 19.3) shows the clamp is active. A varnished picture photographed off-specular can go to about L\* 7-10, which is a large and visible difference for a stormy dark passage. Whichever of the two applies is controlled by the varnish surface roughness, which Berns and de la Rie found matters far more than the varnish's refractive index. **[I]/[computed]**

**Calibration recipe for oilpaint's units (inference).** Choose a physical width W_mm for 1 cw (say a 600 mm painting). Then the weave pitch of 0.7 mm is about 1.2e-3 cw, weave relief of about 0.15 mm about 2.5e-4 cw, a bristle ridge of 0.03-0.1 mm about 5e-5 to 1.7e-4 cw, and heavy impasto of 1 mm about 1.7e-3 cw. These are 0.03% to 0.17% of the canvas width, so at 2400 px across a 600 mm painting one pixel is 0.25 mm. The weave is then about three pixels per thread. Bristle-ridge *heights* (30-100 micrometres) are far below a pixel, while their *widths* are not: a measured brush-mark width of 0.6 mm is about 2.4 px, which matches the "parallel ridges 1-3 px wide at 2400" that `docs/STYLE_REFERENCE.md` gives for `ridge`. That is a useful consistency check on the current lane pitch. The ratios shift with painting size, so the scene spec should carry a physical size. **[I]**

**Reading (inference).** The learned painters are mostly about **stroke placement**: fewer strokes, better fidelity, speed. **Reading (inference).** Four findings translate into settings. (1) Highlights should be sharper and higher in contrast than a physically neutral render would give, and they should sit on the crests of the same relief that shades the diffuse term (Delanoy, Kim). (2) Local texture variation should be 2-5 times what a smooth render gives (Delanoy), with variation in mark direction and placement (Isenberg). (3) Bump amplitude and gloss raise each other's perceived strength, so tune them together (Ho, Hansmann-Roth). (4) Hue and chroma, and stroke coarseness, dominate perceived style (Zhao).

**Suggested test for oilpaint (inference).** A small A/B set: 20 native-resolution crops from open museum scans (Rijksmuseum, Met CC0) against 20 oilpaint crops of matching content and size, at 100% zoom and at print size; ask "real or rendered" and "what gave it away". The 2AFC format of Jiang et al. (14 participants) is a workable protocol. Repeat after each realism change. Add two statistics measured on both sets: the edge-sharpness distribution and the dark-value histogram. Lost-and-found edges and eye-tracking evidence for brushwork were not found; that search was not run before the budget ended.

## 5. How real painters get realism in oil, and what an automatic painter must imitate

Tags: **[V]** = read on the cited page or PDF; **[S]** = seen only in a search snippet; **[I]** = my inference or synthesis. The mapping to oilpaint's parameters (`hgain`, `relief`, `glaze`, `dry_after`, `hardness`, `spill`, `L_floor`, `max_cover`) comes from `docs/STYLE_REFERENCE.md`.

The pattern that matters most: a photographic look in oil comes from **decisions that vary across the picture** (thick here, thin there, hard here, lost there, glossy here, sunken-in there), not from a good brush model applied uniformly. oilpaint's brush and light model are uniform by default, and most knobs are per region or per layer, not tied to the tone or the focus of the picture. **[I]**

| 13 | **Economy of strokes and a consistent hand** | Fewer, decisive strokes; the same loaded brush makes several strokes in a row with a slowly drifting colour | Hertzmann survey (real painting uses long, curved strokes; deterministic algorithms do not reproduce a painter's variability) **[V]** | palette snap, jitter, flecks | Brush memory and paint piles (planned L10) plus a stroke-size distribution with a few large decisive marks and a heavy tail of small ones **[I]** |

### What this section implies for the roadmap **[I]**

1. Three planner-only changes give visible gains before any new physics: tone-coupled relief (row 9), a focus-driven edge budget (row 5) and a deliberate value range (row 10).
2. Rows 2, 3, 4 and 8 all depend on the same missing object, **a paint film with optical layering and a wet/dry state**. The F0 film spike and L11 are the right place for it. The research below supports treating it as the highest-value physics item after lighting.
3. Row 11 (varnish) and the gloss variation in row 3 are absent from every milestone in `LIBRARY_PLAN.md`.

### 6.2 How paintings are actually photographed (what a viewer's eye has learned)

- **Cross-polarised lighting** removes surface glare: a linear polariser on each lamp and a circular polariser on the camera rotated 90 degrees until the reflection is extinguished. Reflected light keeps its polarisation and diffuse light does not, so the result has saturated colour with no varnish sparkle. **[V]** (CHS Open Source pages). Delft's reproduction study photographed paintings under identical mirror-angle lighting with and without a polariser to separate diffuse colour from reflections **[V]**.
- **Two-lamp copy setup:** two softboxes at 45 degrees, equal distance and power, camera on a tripod with the sensor parallel to the painting (avoids keystone), ISO 100, f/8, RAW; check evenness with a white card. Artefacts to avoid: glare on dark glossy works, uneven light, vignetting and perspective distortion. **[V]** (Digital Camera World).
- **Raking light** (grazing, typically about 5-30 degrees from the surface, **[I]**; the page gives no angle) is the documentation view for relief and condition. **[V]** (CHS Open Source).
- **Archival standards** describe capture, not the everyday photo look: FADGI 2016 for paintings asks for at least 600 ppi, illuminance non-uniformity below 1% for the top star level (3%, 5%, above 8% for the lower ones), and mean colour error delta E2000 below 2 (4, 6, 10 for the lower ones); it warns that specular reflections obscure detail and that artworks are rarely truly 2D. Metamorfoze asks for delta E76 at most 10 (maximum) and 4 (mean). **[V]/[S]**.
- **Reflectance transformation imaging (RTI) and photometric stereo** record craquelure, weave, planar distortion and pentimenti from a dome or a moving light and are used in place of raking light for documentation. **[S]** (Malzbender and cultural-heritage sources; the Heritage 2025 review at https://doi.org/10.3390/heritage8040129).
- The **Relievo** replicas (Fujifilm and the Van Gogh Museum, 2013) and **Elevated Printing** (Canon and Factum Arte; layers of 2-4 micrometres, relief up to 5 mm) combine a 2D+3D scan with printing; they show that colour, height and gloss are three separate maps of one painting. **[V]/[S]**.

### 6.3 The stages a "virtual photo studio" would need (all deterministic on CPU) **[I]**

1. **Multi-light relief:** a key raking light plus a broad fill plus ambient occlusion from the height field (section 4.4). Cast shadows by a per-row sweep along the light direction; occlusion by 8-16 horizon sweeps. Only `+ - * /` and `sqrt` are needed.
2. **Surface BRDF per pixel:** roughness and gloss from paint state (wet age, thickness, oil content) and a global varnish level; a Fresnel term (Schlick) so grazing ridges and edges brighten. The surface term is added to the Kubelka-Munk colour by the Saunderson form with separate specular and diffuse parts, so a glossy pass darkens the off-specular colour. Keep gloss as its own map, separate from the height map (Delft).
3. **Highlights tuned for perception:** sharper and higher in contrast than a neutral render, and on the same relief that shades the diffuse term (section 4.8).
4. **Reflection of a soft box:** a small rectangular emitter gives the smooth hotspot and the shape of glare on ridges; a fixed emitter and a fixed camera keep it deterministic.
5. **Canvas as a physical object:** weave at the calibrated millimetre scale (section 4.6), thin-paint transparency so weave and ground show, an unpainted margin, cusping at the edges, a gallery-wrap side and a frame shadow as a compositing step.
6. **Camera:** vignette from the light falloff, slight edge softness, a fixed-seed noise from the engine's counter RNG (shot plus read noise, variance linear in signal), a mild sharpening step. Depth of field only matters for macro crops. Published building blocks (all CPU-friendly; Crossref-verified to exist, not read): Kolb, Mitchell and Hanrahan, *A realistic camera model for computer graphics*, SIGGRAPH 1995; Hullin et al., *Physically-based real-time lens flare rendering*, TOG 2011; Foi et al., *Practical Poissonian-Gaussian noise modeling*, IEEE TIP 2008; Newson et al., film grain, CGF and IPOL 2017; Brooks et al., *Unprocessing images for learned raw denoising*, CVPR 2019 (arXiv 1811.11127, abstract read) **[V]/[S]**. A learned photorealism enhancer (Richter et al. 2021, arXiv 2105.04619) exists but is not deterministic **[V]**.
7. **Ageing (optional):** varnish yellowing as a colour transform on the display, and a crack network (below).

Everything except the noise is a function of the height, wet, coverage and colour planes the engine already has, so the whole stage can live in `oil-light` and be hashed in the golden files. I found no paper on making a render look like a photograph of a painting specifically. **[I]**

### 6.4 Craquelure and ageing (optional but decisive for "an old painting")

- Wikipedia's *Craquelure* article separates **drying cracks** (shallow, in the top layers, within days) from **aging cracks** (sharper, deeper, over the life of the painting; humidity and temperature cycling, oxidation and support movement). Regional character differs: Italian panel paintings show cracks perpendicular to the wood grain with jagged lines; French paintings show non-directional smooth curved cracks. Seven descriptors are used to analyse a network: direction, relation to support texture, shape, spacing, thickness, termination and network organisation. Genuine networks are irregular; induced cracks tend to look uniform. **[V]**
- Classification and mechanism (Crossref-verified to exist, not read): Bucklow, *Studies in Conservation* 42:129 (1997) and 44:233 (1999); Bury 2024 and Antropov 2024, *Heritage Science*; Krzemien 2016 (panels). **[S]**
- Generators (Crossref-verified to exist, not read): Hirota, Tanoue, Kaneko, *Generation of crack patterns with a physical model*, The Visual Computer 1998; Gobron and Chiba, cellular-automaton cracks, 2001; Iben and O'Brien, *Generating surface crack patterns*, Graphical Models 2009 (stress-field based; my memory of its approach is that it suits drying paint and glaze **[I]**); Takato and Nishidate, L-system cracks 2018. Others: a quasi-static phase-field model of a brittle layer (Negri, arXiv 1910.11726) **[S]**; synthetic craquelure for restoration networks (arXiv 2602.12742) **[V]**; cracks as graphs (Sidorov and Hardeberg, arXiv 1905.05010) **[S]**. I found no paper on synthesising craquelure for a digital oil painting.
- **Dust and dulling:** the time-varying BRDF paper gives a dust model in which glossy highlights attenuate exponentially with optical thickness faster than diffuse colour, `(1 - T) rho_dust + T rho_d + rho_s(tau)` with `T = exp(-tau (1/cos theta_i + 1/cos theta_r))`; a cheap way to dull varnished areas. **[V]** (Sun et al. 2007, read by the optics helper)
- **Yellowing:** damar and mastic change colour within 25-50 years (**[V]**, Conservation Wiki); no b\* numbers found, so use a tunable transform.
- My proposal for oilpaint: a crack network computed once from a Voronoi-like tessellation whose cell size follows local paint thickness (thicker film, larger cells; a general fracture-mechanics tendency, **[I]**, not verified here), a direction bias from the canvas weave, darkened crack floors with dust, and a break in the specular highlight at each crack. Deterministic given a seed and the counter RNG; avoid hash-map iteration order. Keep it a switch: it is wrong for a new painting.
